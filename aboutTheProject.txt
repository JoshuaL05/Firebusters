Warehouse fires spread fast. Sprinkler systems are slow to trigger and soak everything at once, so a small fire in one aisle can mean water damage across the whole building. We wanted something that finds a fire early, goes straight to it, and puts out only what is burning, starting with the fire that is most dangerous.

FireBusters is a gantry, built like a 3D printer frame, that hangs over a floor area. A thermal camera on the frame watches the whole floor. When something gets hot, the system ranks every hot spot by size and how fast it is growing, drives a nozzle over the worst one, and checks it with a flame sensor before spraying. That second check matters: a hot mug or a heater looks like a fire to a thermal camera, but it gives off no flame. Once the flame is gone, it confirms the fire is really out, then rescans, because the other fires may have grown in the meantime.

We split the work across two boards. A Raspberry Pi 4 reads the MLX90640 thermal camera, finds and tracks fires with OpenCV, and makes every decision. An ESP32 drives the two stepper motors, presses the trigger, and reads the flame sensor. The ESP32 also has a watchdog: if the Pi stops talking to it for one second, it shuts off the spray and stops the gantry on its own. For the demo, candles stand in for fires and shaving cream is the extinguisher.

To make it feel like a real building system, we split the floor into sectors like A1 and B2, added a live web view anyone on the network can open, voice announcements in the browser, a fire log with response times, and a phone call that goes out once per incident.

Our biggest lesson was to build a simulator early. Our thermal camera arrived late, so we wrote a fake world with fake fires, a fake gantry and a fake ESP32 that plug into the same code. That let us test the whole decision loop before any hardware worked, and it caught real bugs. In one, the system kept switching between two fires, because the fire it was spraying shrank and suddenly looked less important than the other one.

Most of our challenges came from the physical world. Our design changed as hardware showed up: we first planned a pan-tilt arm, then rebuilt around the gantry once it was assembled, and dropped a temperature sensor that could not notice a few candles in an open room. Melted wax stays hot after the flame is out, so the camera kept thinking candles were still burning. We fixed that by trusting the flame sensor to decide when a fire is out. The carriage also blocks the camera’s view when it sits over a fire, so the system parks out of the way before scanning. Smaller problems took time too, like pin conflicts on our ESP32 board and a phone service trial that would not play our own message.

We learned how to split work between a computer and a microcontroller, why safety has to be designed in from the start, and how much you can test before the hardware is ready.
